# NSSDC 1971-063C — Apollo 15 Lunar Module / ALSEP

## Identification

- **NSSDCA/COSPAR ID:** 1971-063C
- **Official catalog name:** Apollo 15 Lunar Module /ALSEP
- **Other official alternate names:** Falcon; LM-10; Apollo 15C; Apollo 15 LM/ALSEP; LEM 15; 05366; Rover 15; Apollo15ALSEP; Apollo 15 Lunar Surface Experiments Package. [1]

## Mission and program

The spacecraft was the Apollo 15 lunar module (LM), named **Falcon**, together with the Apollo Lunar Surface Experiments Package (ALSEP) that it transported and deployed. It was the fourth crewed vehicle to land on the Moon. The mission type was a **crewed lunar landing and surface-exploration mission with geologic fieldwork, sample return, and deployment of long-lived lunar science instruments**. NASA’s NSSDCA lists NASA’s Office of Space Science and NASA’s Office of Manned Space Flight as the funding agencies. [1]

The catalog gives the launch date as **1971-07-26**. It identifies the launch vehicle as Saturn 5 and the launch site as Cape Canaveral, United States. [1]

## Objective

Apollo 15’s LM objective was to land Commander David R. Scott and LM pilot James B. Irwin in the Mare Imbrium region at the foot of the Apennine Mountains, support three lunar EVAs, and enable extensive geological exploration around Hadley Rille. The LM also carried the Lunar Roving Vehicle (LRV), scientific and sample-collection equipment, and ALSEP instruments intended to remain on the surface and transmit scientific data to Earth. The surface work included geologic sample collection, photography, soil-mechanics investigations, and collection of solar-wind particles for return to Earth. [1]

## Key achievements

- Falcon landed on **30 July 1971 at 22:16:29 UT** at 26.13239° N, 3.63330° E, in the Mare Imbrium region. [1]
- Scott and Irwin completed three moonwalk EVAs totaling **18 hours 35 minutes**, traveled **27.9 km**, and collected **77.31 kg** of rock and soil samples. [1]
- Apollo 15 was the first Apollo mission to use a lunar roving vehicle. The LRV explored areas within 5 km of the LM landing site and was used on traverses totaling 10.3 km, 12.5 km, and 5.1 km. [1]
- The crew deployed the Apollo 15 ALSEP. Its instrument suite included a passive seismometer, lunar surface magnetometer, solar-wind spectrometer, suprathermal ion detector, cold-cathode ion gauge, lunar dust detector, and heat-flow experiment. [1]
- The LM lifted off the Moon on **2 August 1971 at 17:11:22 UT**, docked with the CSM, and was later jettisoned and deliberately impacted the Moon. [1]

## Key specifications available in the NSSDC record

- **Total LM mass:** 16,430 kg, including ascent and descent stages and propellants. [1]
- **Ascent-stage dry mass:** 2,385 kg; ascent-stage propellant: 2,375 kg. [1]
- **Descent-stage dry mass:** 2,797 kg, including stowed surface equipment; initial descent-stage propellant: 8,873 kg. [1]
- **Descent stage:** octagonal prism, 4.2 m across and 1.7 m thick; footpads on opposite legs were 9.4 m apart; the stage held the LM approximately 1.5 m above the surface. [1]
- **Ascent stage:** approximately 2.8 m high and 4.0 by 4.3 m wide; pressurized crew-compartment volume 6.65 m³. [1]
- **Propulsion:** descent engine maximum thrust about 45,000 N; ascent engine thrust about 15,000 N; each of four reaction-control thrust modules had four 450 N thrust chambers. [1]
- **Power and communications:** six silver-zinc batteries powered the LM; S-band supported telemetry, TV, voice, and ranging; VHF supported local/CSM communications. [1]
- **ALSEP central station:** 25 kg; stowed volume 34,800 cm³; 58 cm long, 3.8 cm diameter modified axial-helical antenna. Apollo 15 ALSEP downlink frequency was 2278.0 MHz. [1]
- **ALSEP RTG:** SNAP-27; approximately 17 kg; about 70 W DC at 16 V initially and 63.5 W after one year. [1]

## Status

The Apollo 15 lunar-module mission is **completed and inactive**. Falcon remained on the lunar surface for 66 hours 55 minutes, lifted off on 2 August 1971, and after rendezvous and docking its ascent stage was jettisoned on 3 August 1971 at 01:04:14 UT; the catalog records lunar impact at 03:03:37.0 UT at 26.36° N, 0.25° E. The Apollo 15 ALSEP central station operated from 31 July 1971 until the ALSEP network was shut down on 30 September 1977. [1]

## Interesting facts

1. Apollo 15’s LRV was the first vehicle of its type used on the Moon. [1]
2. After the final EVA, Scott performed the televised hammer-and-feather demonstration, showing that both fell at the same rate in the lunar vacuum. [1]
3. The crew left a plaque and small figure commemorating fourteen American and Soviet space explorers who had died during the two nations’ space programs. [1]

## 3D model search

NASA’s official 3D Resources catalog provides a downloadable **Apollo Lunar Module.glb**. The NASA page identifies the source as **NASA/Michael D. Carbajal**, gives the file size as **700.04 KB**, and links the asset to NASA’s official `NASA-3D-Resources` GitHub repository. The repository directory lists the exact file name `Apollo Lunar Module.glb`. [2] [3]

The model is a credible official Apollo LM representation, but the NASA page does **not** identify it as the Apollo 15 vehicle Falcon, LM-10, or as an LM/ALSEP combination. Therefore it should not be represented as a verified spacecraft-specific reconstruction. NASA’s separate Apollo 15 asset is `Apollo 15 - Landing Site.stl`, a 30 × 30 km height-map/terrain model of the Apennine Mountains, not a model of Falcon or ALSEP, so it is not included as a spacecraft candidate. [4]

NASA’s 3D Resources hub states that its assets are free to download and use and directs users to NASA’s Images and Media Usage Guidelines. Those guidelines state that NASA content used in the rendition of 3D models, including texture maps and polygon data, is generally not subject to copyright in the United States and may be used for educational or informational purposes, subject to attribution, no implied NASA endorsement, and third-party-rights limitations. The NASA data catalog separately says “License not specified,” so the catalog record should not be treated as a standalone license grant. [5] [6]

The NASA data catalog gives **100,628 polygons and 60,845 vertices** for the legacy Apollo Lunar Module dataset and exposes a legacy 3DS ZIP download. Because the modern NASA Science page identifies the downloadable GLB but does not publish a polygon count, the polygon figure is reported as metadata for the related legacy dataset, not as a verified count of the GLB file itself. [3]

### Model status
