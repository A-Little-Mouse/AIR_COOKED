# NSSDC 1969-099C — Apollo 12 Lunar Module / ALSEP

## Mission identification

The sole mission-information source used for this record is NASA’s National Space Science Data Center/NSSDCA spacecraft entry for COSPAR ID **1969-099C**. The official catalog name is **Apollo 12 Lunar Module / ALSEP**. The lunar module itself was named **Intrepid**. [1]

- **Launch date:** 1969-11-14 (14 November 1969). [1]
- **Agency/program:** NASA — Office of Manned Space Flight (United States) and NASA — Office of Space Science (United States). [1]
- **Mission type:** Lunar landing / Moon exploration. [1]
- **Launch vehicle/site:** Saturn 5 from Cape Canaveral, United States. [1]

## Objective

The Apollo 12 lunar module was intended to land two astronauts on the Moon and support surface exploration. The NSSDC description specifies geological sample collection, surface photography, soil-mechanics investigations of the lunar regolith, a solar-wind composition experiment, deployment of the Apollo Lunar Surface Experiments Package (ALSEP), and collection of parts from the previously landed Surveyor 3 spacecraft. [1]

## Key achievements

- The module landed on 19 November 1969 in Oceanus Procellarum at 3.0128° S, 23.4219° W, within about 180 m of Surveyor 3. [1]
- Charles P. “Pete” Conrad and Alan L. Bean completed two lunar surface excursions totaling 7 hours 45 minutes. The catalog records a 1.35 km total traverse and a 1.3 km geology traverse on the second excursion. [1]
- The astronauts returned **34.35 kg** of lunar rock and soil and brought back approximately **10 kg** of Surveyor 3 parts for examination on Earth. [1]
- They deployed the Apollo 12 ALSEP, including a passive seismometer, suprathermal ion detector, cold-cathode ion gauge, lunar dust detector, lunar surface magnetometer, and solar-wind spectrometer. [1]
- The intentionally jettisoned ascent stage struck the Moon and produced the first recorded artificial moonquake. [1]

## Key specifications

The following values are those stated in the NSSDC entry; they describe the combined lunar-module stages unless otherwise noted. [1]

- **Total spacecraft mass:** 15,235 kg, including ascent- and descent-stage propellants.
- **Ascent stage:** 2,383 kg dry mass; 2,375 kg propellant; approximately 2.8 m high and 4.0 × 4.3 m wide; crew-compartment volume 6.65 m³.
- **Descent stage:** 2,118 kg dry mass including stowed surface equipment; 8,359 kg initial propellant; octagonal prism approximately 4.2 m across and 1.7 m thick; 9.4 m between opposite footpad ends; the footpads held the stage bottom about 1.5 m above the surface.
- **Propulsion:** Descent engine maximum thrust about 45,000 N; ascent engine thrust about 15,000 N; each of four reaction-control thrust modules contained four 450 N thrust chambers.
- **Power:** Six silver-zinc batteries powered the lunar module. The ALSEP SNAP-27 radioisotope thermoelectric generator produced approximately 70 W DC at 16 V, falling to 63.5 W after one year.
- **Communications and guidance:** S-band handled telemetry, television, voice, and ranging; VHF supported local/CSM communications. Guidance used radar ranging, an inertial measurement unit, and the Apollo guidance computer.
- **ALSEP central station:** 25 kg; stowed volume 34,800 cm³; 2119 MHz uplink and 2278.5 MHz Apollo 12 downlink.

## Status

The lunar module lifted off the Moon on 20 November 1969 after 31 hours 31 minutes on the surface, docked with the command/service module, and was jettisoned and intentionally crashed into the Moon. The ALSEP central station and instruments were shut down on 30 September 1977. Thus, the flight vehicle is no longer operational, while the deployed surface experiment package operated until that 1977 shutdown. [1]

## Interesting facts

1. The Apollo 12 landing was unusually close to an earlier spacecraft: Intrepid came within about 180 m of Surveyor 3, allowing the crew to retrieve parts for terrestrial examination. [1]
2. The lunar-module television camera was accidentally pointed at the Sun during setup. Its vidicon tube was damaged, making the camera inoperable. [1]
3. The ALSEP RTG used plutonium-238 heat and had a stated half-life of 89.6 years for that isotope; the generator’s eight fins radiated excess heat to space. [1]

## Official 3D model search

NASA’s official 3D Resources catalog lists an **Apollo Lunar Module** asset and states that its assets are free to download and use, with users directed to NASA’s Images and Media Usage Guidelines. [2] The individual NASA Science asset page identifies the file as **`Apollo Lunar Module.glb`**, dated 20 March 2025, with a displayed size of **700.04 KB**. [3] The direct asset URL is the download link exposed by that page:

`https://assets.science.nasa.gov/content/dam/science/cds/3d/resources/model/apollo-lunar-module/Apollo%20Lunar%20Module.glb?emrc=6af7ea314608c`

A direct download of that URL measured **716,840 bytes** on disk (approximately 700.04 KiB as displayed by NASA). Parsing the GLB JSON chunk found 134 meshes and 157 primitives, with 292,764 index entries and **97,588 triangles**. NASA does not publish a polygon-count field on the asset page; the triangle count here is therefore a reproducible inspection of the downloaded file, not a NASA-stated specification.

The NASA GitHub mirror confirms the exact model filename in the `3D Models/Apollo Lunar Module` directory. [4] The model is a general Apollo Lunar Module representation. The pages do not identify it as the specific Apollo 12 vehicle **Intrepid**, and the model listing does not state that the ALSEP package is included. It is therefore a clearly licensed official generic Apollo LM asset and a reasonable spacecraft-level match, but not verified as a mission-specific Apollo 12/ALSEP reconstruction.

A review of the official NASA/JPL Horizons news page found no downloadable Apollo lunar-module 3D file or JPL-hosted model listing. [5] Accordingly, no separate JPL model candidate is reported.
